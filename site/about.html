About this model

A teaching sketch of the transit light source effect and of how Pandora's simultaneous visible and infrared observations could help. It is built to show the idea, not to predict a real star, so this page says what is simplified, what was checked, and where the sketch stops being trustworthy.

Suggested walk-through for a group meeting (about 8 minutes)

  1. 0:00 · Page 1, 1½ min. Let the transit play. Drag Spot size from 0 up to about 1.5 and watch the solid curve pull away from the dashed one. The planet is identical in both; only the star changed.
  2. 1:30 · Page 2, 2½ min. Point at the spectrum: the error is larger in the visible than in the infrared, so the whole spectrum tilts. Press Put a spot in the path and the error flips sign, because the planet now blocks a dim spot. Switch M and K: with these presets the errors come out nearly the same.
  3. 4:00 · Page 3, 3½ min. Leave the layout on Typical and press Correct it: the corrected spectrum lands on the truth. Then try Clustered (overcorrects) and Spread (barely corrects, with a narrow range that misses). Finish by raising the visits and lowering the noise to show that they do not fix a wrong assumption.
  4. 7:30 · This page, ½ min. Close on what is simplified, below.

Tip: the Projector view button in the header enlarges text and charts.

The main equation

When the planet's path is free of spots, the measured depth is the true depth times a correction ε that depends on wavelength:

ε(λ) = 1 / [ 1 − f_spot · (1 − S_spot/S_phot) − f_fac · (1 − S_fac/S_phot) ]

f_spot and f_fac are the shares of the star's light taken up by spots and faculae. S is the brightness of spots, faculae and ordinary photosphere at that wavelength. Cool spots have S_spot well below S_phot, so ε is above 1 and the planet looks too big, most of all at the short wavelengths where the contrast is greatest.

If the planet crosses a spot, this shortcut no longer applies. The planet blocks a dim patch, and the dip gets shallower. Page 2 shows that case.

Where the correction breaks

A rotation light curve shows how the star's brightness changes. It does not show how spotted the star is on average, because spots spread evenly around the star hardly change the brightness at all. The page 3 method has to assume a link between the two, and the factor κ encodes it. It was calibrated on 1000 random layouts, and the middle two-thirds of them give κ between about 0.7 and 1.9. Clustered spots fall below that range and evenly spread spots far above it.

That limit is known in the literature: Rackham et al. (2018) found that a large range of spot and faculae covering fractions is consistent with the same variability, and that corrections assuming a linear relation between variability and coverage tend to underestimate the contamination. A full analysis would use more information than this sketch does.

What was checked

  • With no spots, the transit light curve matches the batman package to about 1 ppm, for depths from 2,500 to 22,500 ppm.
  • With spots kept off the planet's path, the pixel model's depth equals the true depth times the analytic ε, to machine precision.
  • The browser code reproduces the Python reference model to about 1e-16 on four saved cases, including an occulted spot and a tilted spin axis.
  • The browser's κ for three named layouts agrees with the Python calibration to three digits.
  • The page 3 method removes the bias for a typical layout, and fails in the documented ways for clustered and spread layouts.

What was not checked

  • Realistic stellar atmosphere spectra. Everything here uses blackbodies.
  • Any comparison with real Pandora data or a full simulation of its performance.
  • Whether the conclusions hold for real spot distributions, which the random-layout prior only approximates.
  • Review by a domain expert, and a cold read by someone who has not seen the site before.
