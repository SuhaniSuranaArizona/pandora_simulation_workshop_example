Pandora's trick: let the visible light describe the star

Pandora watches the star in visible light while it measures the infrared spectrum. As spots rotate in and out of view the visible brightness wobbles, and the size of that wobble says how spotted the star is. That tells us how much to correct the infrared spectrum. The catch is that a wobble only shows change, so turning it into an average needs an assumption. Try the three spot layouts below to see where it works and where it does not.

What to notice

  1. Typical: press Correct it. The measured spectrum sits too deep, and the corrected one lands on the truth, inside its shaded range.
  2. Clustered spots make the visible light wobble a lot for the amount of spottedness, so the method thinks the star is more spotted than it is and overcorrects. Spread spots nearly cancel out as the star turns, so the wobble is tiny and the method corrects almost nothing. In both cases the true κ in the “hidden truth” card falls outside the assumed range.
  3. Watch the shaded range on the Spread star: it is narrow, yet it misses the truth. The range only reflects the assumption and the noise, so when the assumption is wrong the method is confidently wrong. More visits and lower noise tighten the fit but cannot cure that.
How the estimate works, and what it assumes

Fit a sinusoid at the known rotation period to the visible data to get the modulation amplitude. Multiply it by a factor κ to estimate the star's average dimming at 0.6 µm, then use the known spot and star temperatures to scale that to each infrared wavelength and divide each transit by its ε.
